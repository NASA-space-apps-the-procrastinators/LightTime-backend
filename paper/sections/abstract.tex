At Mars, a report from a spacecraft takes minutes to reach Earth and a reply takes twice as long: on 14 November 2026 the one-way light time was \numMarsOneWayS~s and the round trip \numMarsRoundTripS~s. A flight surgeon on Earth cannot direct care for a vital sign that will turn dangerous sooner than that. We describe LIGHT-TIME, a crew health monitor built for the NASA Space Apps Challenge 2026. Its ship computer decides who acts, the flight surgeon on Earth or the crew medical officer on board, from the one-way light time, and switches at \numCrewFirstAtS~s by design choice. For a falling blood-oxygen reading it fits a Theil-Sen slope, keeps the slope only when Kendall's tau shows that most pairs of readings fall, and compares the time left with the round trip. Implausible readings are discarded before any band or trend is judged, and no trained model decides. We replayed public recordings through the same rules. All \numUwEvents{} recordings of induced low oxygen in healthy volunteers were warned before the SOS, by a median of \numUwMedianLeadS~s (range \numUwMinLeadS{} to \numUwMaxLeadS~s), and settings chosen with each recording left out warned on \numHeldoutWarned{} of \numUwEvents. In most recordings a fixed band was already held when the warning came, so the trend rule mainly added a stated deadline. On a healthy \numControlHours-hour night the trust rules cut false SOS alarms from \numControlFalseSosRaw{} to \numControlFalseSosSettle. The evidence is \numUwEvents{} laboratory events on Earth. It shows that the parts work as designed, not that the system helps a crew. Code, tests and the scripts behind every number are in the project repository.
\end{abstract}
